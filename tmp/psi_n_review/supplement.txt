B.2 Additional Data Applications
We also apply the proposed method to two more data set, i.e., Boston Housing and Concrete
Compressive Strength data.
B.2.1 Concrete Compressive Strength Data
We apply the proposed methodology, along with the competing methods, to the Concrete
Compressive Strength Data Set (Yeh, 1998) available on the UCI machine learning repos-
itory. The data consist of n = 1030 observations, with the response being a continuous
variable of concrete compressive strength (CCS), and eight covariates: cement ( Z1), water
(Z2), ﬂy ash (Z3), blast furnace slag ( Z4), superplasticizer (Z5), coarse aggregate (Z6), ﬁne
aggregate (Z7) and age of the mixture in days (Z8), of which the ﬁrst seventh covariates are
the ingredients in high-performance concrete (HPC). For concrete technology, the water-
cement ratios (WCR) has been recognized as the most useful and signiﬁcant advancement
for CCS (Yeh, 1998). Here we not only explore the association between WCR and CCS,
but also predict the CCS of HPC from the covariates.
Similar to the analysis in Natality Birth and Boston Housing Data, we model the data
with four approaches: LQR, PLAQR, DPLQR and DNQR. Here we treat log CCS as the

B.2.2 Boston Housing Data
The Boston Housing Data, available from the R package mlbench, is a benchmark dataset
for quantile regression analysis. In Harrison and Rubinfeld (1978), 506 observations were
examined to study the housing prices based on various demographic and socioeconomic
predictors. The variables are: median house price ( Y ), per capita crime rate by town (X1),
a river boundary indicator ( X2), proportion of non-retail business acres per town ( X3),
proportion of residential land zoned for lots ( X4), nitrogen oxides concentration ( X5),
average number of rooms per dwelling (X6), proportion of owner-occupied units built prior
to 1940 ( X7), weighted mean of distances to ﬁve Boston employment centers ( X8), index
15

PDF PAGE 16
of accessibility to radial highways ( X9), full-value property-tax rate ( X10), pupil-teacher
ratio by town ( X11), the proportion of black individuals by town ( X12), the percentage of
the population classiﬁed as lower status ( X13).
Since the work of Thaler (1978), economists have tried to understand the impact of
crime rate on housing price. To address this question, we model X1 (per capita crime rate
by town) as the parametric predictor to study the eﬀect of the crime rates on house price.
All other covariates are treated as the nonparametric predictors, i.e.,
log Y = θ1X1 + m(X2, X3, . . . , X13) + ϵ.
We also consider the linear quantile regression (LQR), partially linear additive quantile
regression (PLAQR) and the deep nonparametric quantile regression [DNQR, see Padilla
et al. (2020) and Shen et al. (2021)], which treats all thirteen covariates as components
of a nonparametric regression model, i.e., log Y = m(X1, X2, X3, . . . , X13) + ϵ. Note that
DNQR does not oﬀer direct treatment eﬀects.
The estimates ˆθ1 from the quantile levels τ∈ [0.02, 0.98] are shown in Figure 7, revealing
that the crime rate has a signiﬁcant eﬀect on the price of a house and house prices are higher
in areas with lower crime rates. Figure 8 displays the prediction error in terms of check loss
on randomly hold-out test set (20% of data). It shows the proposed method is considerably
better than the LQR and PLADR methods and is comparable to DNQR.
